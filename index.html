Abstract

I spent Summer 2026 at Deloitte as a Business Systems Analyst on the Tech and Transformation team, staffed on an engagement helping a home and auto insurance client migrate their legacy rating and analysis logic onto Earnix, a modern rating and analytics platform used across the insurance industry. Over four months I moved from shadowing existing work to owning pieces of it end to end - building rating structures, running requirement-gathering sessions directly with the client, and testing my own development before it shipped. What I'll want a reader to take away is that this term wasn't a "junior analyst" job in the traditional sense: on a small, generalist team, everyone does everything, and that structure changed how I think about what a business analyst actually is.

About Deloitte

Deloitte is one of the world's largest professional services firms, and I sat within its Tech and Transformation practice - the group that helps clients modernize the systems their business actually runs on, rather than building products of Deloitte's own. My engagement placed me with a home and auto insurance client working through a multi-year migration of their pricing and rating logic off an aging, in-house system and onto Earnix, a purpose-built rating and analytics platform used widely across the insurance industry.

The most interesting thing about how my specific team runs isn't the client or the software - it's that there's no separation between "developers," "testers," and "business analysts." Everyone on the team gathers requirements, builds, tests, and demos their own work, which meant I was never just handed a fully scoped ticket and told to code it.

Learning outcomes for this term

Going into the term, I knew this role would push me outside a purely technical comfort zone, so most of my goals were about operating well on a client-facing, cross-functional team rather than learning a single tool.

  • ✓
    Operate at a high level in a team environment without sacrificing quality Because our team doesn't split dev, testing, and elaboration across different people, I had to learn to move quickly through a story while still testing it rigorously — rating errors directly affect what a real customer gets charged, so "quick" and "careless" couldn't be the same thing.
  • ✓
    Own requirements from elaboration through to a working build By mid-term I was running elaboration sessions with the client directly on new epics, making sure requirements were fully understood on both sides before any development started, then building and testing that same work myself.
  • ~
    Speak confidently in important meetings and demo work to non-technical leaders I got real reps presenting completed development to client stakeholders and explaining technical decisions in plain terms, but I'm still working on staying just as concise and confident when a senior leader pushes back on the spot rather than just asking clarifying questions.
  • ✓
    Get comfortable in the Earnix platform I started the term shadowing existing rating structures and rules and finished it building new pre-rating rules independently, which is the clearest evidence of progress I have from the whole term.

What the job actually looked like

My core project was helping migrate a home and auto insurance client's rating and analysis logic off an outdated legacy system and onto Earnix. Practically, that meant rebuilding pre-rating rules and rating structures that already existed in the old system inside the new platform — not from scratch conceptually, but faithfully and correctly, which turned out to be its own kind of hard: it's easy to build something that looks right and much harder to prove it behaves identically to a system that's been running in production for years.

On a given day I could be developing an already-defined pre-rating rule inside Earnix, demoing finished work directly to the client, gathering requirements in depth on a brand-new epic, running an elaboration session to make sure both sides agreed on what "done" meant before a line of code was written, or fully testing a development story someone on my team had built. The skill that surprised me most wasn't technical - it was learning to ask the right clarifying question in an elaboration session, since a vague requirement caught early costs an hour of conversation, but caught late costs a rebuild.

"How does it handle this edge case?" a client stakeholder asked mid-demo. I already knew — I'd written the test for it the week before.

Conclusions

If someone described this report in one sentence, I'd want it to be that I spent a term learning that "business analyst" doesn't mean staying out of the technical weeds - on a lean, generalist team, understanding the business need and building the correct rating logic are the same skill, not two separate ones. Going into my next term, I'm more comfortable owning a piece of work fully, from the first client conversation about what they need through to a tested, working result.
